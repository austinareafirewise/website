Preview — this is the real page with your item shown in place.

This is where everything a visitor reads on the website comes from: the meeting notice at the top of the home page, and the news articles.

The two kinds

  • Meeting notice — a short band across the top of the home page, above the big headline. For a meeting, a chipper day, a seminar: something happening on a date. Keep it to a few sentences.
  • News article — a full page of its own on the News page, with room for pictures. Tick Show the title in the News section on the home page and the headline also appears on the home page.

Draft, Preview, Publish

  1. Save as draft keeps your work. Visitors never see a draft. A draft can be unfinished — no title yet, no dates yet, nothing but a picture you wanted to keep. Save it and come back.
  2. Preview opens the real page with your item in place, exactly as a visitor would see it. Use it every time.
  3. Publish puts it on the site. This is the step that insists on a title, some text and both dates.

Take off the site turns a published item back into a draft — it is not deleted. Delete removes it for good, and asks first. Duplicate makes a copy to start the next one from, which is the easy way to do a monthly meeting notice.

Show from, Show until

Both are required to publish, and both are Central time. The item appears on the site at the first time and disappears at the second, on its own — nobody has to remember to take it down. A published item whose start time has not arrived shows in the list as Scheduled.

Allow up to a minute for a change to reach the public site.

Pictures

News articles take up to three. Drop one on the dotted box, drag the frame to choose what shows, and say what is in the picture — that sentence is what someone using a screen reader hears instead of seeing it, so it is required.

Each picture sits either across the whole width or beside the text, left or right, with the writing flowing round it. Pictures are spread down the article in the order you add them: the first above the first paragraph, the next a couple of paragraphs below. You can change where one sits at any time from its card — no need to upload it again. On a phone every picture goes full width, because a picture and two words beside it is worse than either alone.

If a photo comes out on its side, use Turn left or Turn right above the frame — each press is a quarter turn, and the frame goes back to the middle afterwards. A picture already on the article can be turned too: use Rotate / re-crop on its card and it is replaced where it stands, keeping its description, credit and position.

Only the cropped part is kept, and it is re-made on your own computer before it is sent, which strips the location and camera information phones hide inside photos.

Everyone who asked to hear from us: from the sign-up form on the home page, or added here by hand.

How somebody joins

Filling in the form is not enough on its own. We send one message asking them to confirm, and nothing else is ever sent until they do. That is a legal requirement for email and for texts, and it is also what keeps our messages out of spam folders. Somebody who signed up but has not confirmed shows as Pending; if they never confirm, their record is deleted automatically after a week.

What the statuses mean

  • Confirmed — they answered the confirmation. They receive messages.
  • Pending — signed up, has not confirmed yet. They receive nothing. Use Resend if they say it never arrived, and tell them to look in their spam folder.
  • Unsubscribed — they asked to stop, or replied STOP to a text. Their record is kept on purpose, so that a later form submission cannot quietly put them back on the list.
  • Bounced — the address does not exist, or rejected us permanently. Nothing more is sent there. Continuing to mail addresses that bounce is what gets a whole domain blocked.

Adding someone by hand

Only for somebody who actually asked — a paper sign-up sheet at a meeting, or a request in person. The form makes you write down how consent was obtained, and that sentence is stored with their record as the evidence. They still get the confirmation message; nobody can be marked confirmed by hand.

Never add an address from a directory, a neighbourhood list, or another organisation's roster. That is what the law calls an imported list, and one complaint from it can cost us the ability to send anything at all.

Unsubscribe, or delete?

Unsubscribe when they ask to stop hearing from us: messages stop immediately and the record stays, so they cannot be re-added by accident. Delete only when they ask to be erased entirely — it removes the record and the consent evidence with it.

The other buttons

Search matches name, email, phone and neighbourhood. Download CSV gives you the whole list as a spreadsheet — it is personal information, so keep it off shared drives and delete your copy when you are done with it.

Email and text messages to the membership. Nothing here is sent by one person alone: somebody writes it, somebody else approves it, and only then can it go.

The order things happen

  1. Draft — write it. Only you see it. Edit it as often as you like.
  2. Send me a test — sends the real message to you alone. Do this before every submission. It is the only way to see what it actually looks like on a phone.
  3. Submit for review — hands it to the rest of the board. You can still Withdraw it.
  4. Approve — done by a different board member. You cannot approve your own message; the button is not there for the person who wrote it. Anyone else can Request changes instead, with a note, which sends it back as a draft.
  5. Send now — the last step, and it asks first.

A message that has gone out cannot be recalled, corrected or deleted. Read it once more before pressing Send.

Who it goes to

Email subscribers, text subscribers, or everyone. People chose which when they signed up, so someone who only wanted email never gets a text. Unconfirmed, unsubscribed and bounced people are skipped automatically.

Writing it

  • Name is for your own list. It is never sent.
  • Email subject and Email message — the message accepts Markdown. The unsubscribe link and our postal address are added for you; both are required by law and neither can be removed.
  • Text message — short. "Reply STOP to stop" is added for you. Watch the count shown beside the box: a long text is billed and delivered as several, and links and emoji eat the allowance fast.
  • Send at — leave it blank to send when approved, or set a time. Texts should land between 8am and 9pm; outside those hours is both rude and against the rules.

Afterwards

A sent message stays in the list with the date it went and who approved it. Duplicate starts a new draft from it, which is how to do a recurring reminder without retyping it.

The neighbourhood groups the Alliance works with. This list is also the dropdown people choose from on the public sign-up form, so what you write here is read by residents.

Status, and what it changes

  • Prospective — interested, nothing started yet.
  • In progress — working towards recognition.
  • Recognized Firewise USA site — recognised, and current. Put the year in; it is required for this status.
  • Lapsed — was recognised, the renewal is overdue.
  • Inactive — hides the group from the sign-up form. Use this rather than deleting a group that no longer meets: deleting loses its history.

Filling it in

Area it covers should read the way a resident would describe where they live — streets, a subdivision, "everything north of the creek" — not a formal boundary. That is the line somebody uses to work out whether the group is theirs. ZIP codes are comma separated. Households is the number the group counts for its Firewise paperwork.

Leadership

Add the people who actually run the group, with a role and a way to reach them. This is the Alliance's own record of who to call, not a public directory — nothing in the group record is shown on the website except the group's name in the sign-up dropdown.

Renewal due

Firewise USA recognition has to be renewed. Put the date here so the board can see what is coming; a group that goes past it should be moved to Lapsed until it is renewed.

Deleting a group

People who chose that group when they signed up are not deleted with it — their records are kept and simply lose the group. The dialog tells you how many were affected.

Who is on the board of the Alliance, with roles and terms. This is the organisation's own record.

A board record is not a login

These two things are deliberately separate. Adding somebody here does not let them edit the website, and removing them does not take that away. Website access is granted and removed separately, by whoever manages the AWS account, so that the roster can be kept accurate by anyone without accidentally handing out or cutting off access.

When somebody leaves the board, do both: set them to Past board member here, and ask whoever manages the account to remove their login.

Current and past

Set Past board member rather than deleting somebody who has served — the record of who held which office and when is worth keeping, and a past member needs an end date. Delete only a record that should never have existed, such as a duplicate.

The other fields

Role is free text — chair, treasurer, secretary, member at large. Their Firewise group is optional and only records which neighbourhood group they belong to, if any; it does not give them anything. Email and phone are for the board's own use and are not published.

Worth ten minutes once. Most questions about "why did it do that" are answered by knowing these few things.

Two separate halves

Editing and reading are deliberately kept apart. What you do here goes through a sign-in, an API and a database. The public website does none of that — it reads a single file of already-published content and some pictures, and nothing else.

That is the reason the site stays up. If this admin area is broken, offline, or being attacked, visitors see no difference at all: the site keeps serving whatever was last published. On a page people may open during an actual fire, having no moving parts on the public side is the property that matters most.

What "published" actually does

Pressing Publish rewrites one file that the public site reads. It contains every published item whose stop date has not yet passed — including ones scheduled for next month. Your browser shows only the ones whose window includes right now, which is why a notice scheduled for next Tuesday appears on Tuesday without anybody doing anything. No timer runs anywhere.

Because scheduled items sit in that public file early, somebody determined enough could read next week's notice a week early. For meetings and chipper days that is harmless. It does mean nothing confidential should ever be typed into this system — not a member's private circumstances, not an address someone wants kept quiet, not anything about an incident. If something must be held back, leave it as a draft: drafts are never in the public file.

The four states an item can be in

  • Draft — saved, never public. Can be previewed.
  • Scheduled — published, start date still to come. It appears on its own.
  • Live — published and inside its window. On the site now.
  • Ended — stop date passed. Hidden from visitors, kept for the record. Delete it when you no longer want it in the list.

Drafts and ended items sit greyed at the bottom of the list. Anything not live is labelled as such, on purpose.

Why a change takes a minute

The published file is cached for about a minute so the site stays fast. A change can take that long to show up for visitors, and refreshing your own browser harder will not hurry it. Preview does not go through the cache, so it is always current.

Where each thing appears

  • Meeting notices — a green band at the top of the home page, above the headline. Several active notices stack, newest first. Green rather than orange because on this site orange means alert, and a meeting is not one.
  • Home News section — titles only, under the headline, for articles ticked Show on home page. It exists to draw attention, not to be the news.
  • News page — every live article, and a page of its own for each one with a shareable web address.

What happens to a picture

Cropping happens on your own computer, before anything is sent. What leaves your machine is the cropped result at two sizes; the original file never does. That has a side effect worth knowing: re-making the picture strips the hidden information phones bury in photos, including GPS coordinates. A board member's photo of a chipper day will not publish the location of their driveway.

Why there is no formatting toolbar

Everything typed here reaches the website as text, never as page code. That is a deliberate wall: it means an editor account in the wrong hands still cannot put anything harmful into the site. Markdown does not change it — the website reads the punctuation itself and builds the headings and lists, and anything it does not recognise is shown exactly as typed. It is also why a meeting notice stays plain.

If something looks wrong

  • A change has not appeared — give it a minute, then check the item is Live and not Draft or Scheduled.
  • An item vanished from the site — its stop date almost certainly passed. It is still in the list, under Ended.
  • Signed in but nothing works — sign out and back in; the sign-in expires after a while.
  • An error you cannot get past — nothing is changed when a save fails, so it is safe to leave it and ask for help.

Things worth knowing

  • Leave a blank line between paragraphs. One line break on its own does nothing — this is the mistake everybody makes first.
  • The # and - must be at the start of the line, with a space after them.
  • Links must start with https://. Anything else is left as plain text.
  • Anything the website does not recognise is shown exactly as you typed it. A stray asterisk is a stray asterisk, not an error.
  • Untick the box and the whole lot appears literally, punctuation and all. If the article is plain sentences, leave it unticked.

Use Preview before publishing. It shows the real page, and it is quicker than wondering.
