Ready · Set · Go

Getting out

Defensible space decides whether a house has a chance. Planning decides whether the people inside it leave in time. In a county full of hill streets with one way out, the second question is the harder one.

First: make sure a warning can reach you

Register with Warn Central Texas

The regional emergency notification system does not know your phone number until you give it one. Register every mobile number in the household, and add the address of anywhere you spend a lot of time. warncentraltexas.org

Do not rely on a single channel

Wireless Emergency Alerts reach phones in an area without registration, but they are coarse. Power and cell service can fail. Keep a battery or crank radio, and agree with neighbors on a phone tree that works when the internet does not.

Register people who will need help

The State of Texas Emergency Assistance Registry (STEAR) collects information about residents who may need assistance in an emergency — mobility, medical equipment, transportation, communication needs. Registering does not guarantee a ride, and planning with neighbors still matters. STEAR registry

Watch conditions yourself

On red-flag days, know where the wind is coming from. Smoke you can see is information, and it may arrive before any alert does.

Ready, Set, Go!

The national framework fire agencies use. The value is that it names three distinct moments, so you are not making every decision at once on the worst day.

Ready

Long before a fire — now.

  • Defensible space and home hardening done
  • Registered for alerts; STEAR if it applies
  • Go-bags packed and where you can grab them
  • Two routes out, and a meeting point outside the area
  • Photos or video of the house and contents, stored off-site
  • Plan for pets and livestock — trailers take time to hitch

Set

A fire is burning nearby. Be ready to move.

  • Bags, medications, documents, and pets in the vehicle
  • Vehicle backed into the driveway, windows up, keys in hand
  • Close windows and doors; leave them unlocked for firefighters
  • Move patio furniture and cushions inside; shut off gas at the appliance
  • Leave outside lights on so the house is visible in smoke
  • Check on neighbors who may not have heard

Go

Leave. Early is the whole point.

  • Go when you feel uneasy — do not wait for an order
  • Take the route away from the smoke, not the familiar one
  • Headlights on, drive slow enough for poor visibility
  • Tell someone outside the area that you are moving
  • Do not go back for things

Leaving early is not overreacting. The danger in a one-exit neighborhood is not only the fire — it is several hundred households deciding to leave in the same ten minutes, on the same road, while apparatus is trying to come the other way. The households that left during "Set" are the reason that road still works.

What goes in the bag

Pack it once. Check it when the clocks change.

  • Medications and medical equipment A week's worth, plus chargers and spare batteries for anything powered.
  • Documents IDs, insurance policies, deed or lease, and a copy of the household plan. Digital copies too.
  • Water and food Three days for each person, and for each animal.
  • Phone, charger, backup battery A car charger matters more than you expect.
  • N95 masks Smoke is the part of a wildfire that reaches far past the fire.
  • Cash and a paper contact list Card readers and saved contacts both assume infrastructure.
  • Sturdy shoes, long pants, cotton or wool layers Not synthetics, which melt.
  • Pet carriers, leashes, and records Shelters ask for vaccination records.

Streets with one way out

Much of Travis County was built up a hill or along a canyon rim, on a road that dead-ends. That geometry is worth planning around explicitly.

Know your second route

Even if it is a greenbelt path on foot, a neighbor's driveway, or a gated utility road. Walk it before you need it, in daylight.

Keep the road usable

Street parking that narrows a road to one lane stops a fire engine. Overhanging limbs below about 13 feet do the same. This is a neighborhood-level fix, not an individual one.

Sort out gates in advance

If an emergency route runs through a locked gate, settle now who holds the key or code and how responders get through. Nobody wants to discover that question at 2 a.m.

Pair up with neighbors

Agree who checks on whom — especially residents who do not drive, use oxygen or powered equipment, or live alone. Keep it voluntary and keep the list with the people on it.

The Alliance has put a detailed set of evacuation questions to area agencies — who orders an evacuation, how fast a message goes out, what happens when the one exit is blocked, how residents needing assistance are accounted for. As answers come back, they are published here rather than staying in a meeting. Get those updates

Coming back

Return only when officials say the area is open. Burned ground hides hot spots and holes, ash can be caustic, downed lines may still be live, and a structure that looks intact can be unsafe. Document damage before cleaning up, and wear an N95 and gloves while you do it.

Wildfire smoke is a health problem at a distance from any flame, particularly for children, older adults, pregnant people, and anyone with heart or lung conditions. Austin Public Health publishes guidance during smoke events.
